Composite vs multi-single-segment: same shape, different rules

What it controls

Two FCFs look almost identical: a composite position callout and a multi-single-segment position callout. Both are two-line stacks. Both have a tighter inner zone than outer zone. They mean very different things.

Composite (one position symbol spanning both rows): the lower line controls feature-to-feature spacing within the pattern; it inherits only the primary datum's orientation — secondary and tertiary datums appear on the lower line for rotation lock only, NOT for translation. The pattern as a whole is free to drift in the plane perpendicular to the primary datum.

Multi-single-segment (a separate position symbol on each row): all datums apply on every line, for both rotation and translation. The pattern is locked to the datums on both lines — the lower line is just a tighter version of the upper.

Look at the diagram

Two stacked FCFs side-by-side, looking nearly identical. Left (composite): one ⌖ symbol spans both rows. The lower-line datum references rotate the pattern but don't pin it in space. Right (multi-single-segment): each row has its own ⌖. Both rows fully constrain.

Below each FCF, a tiny plan view of the four-hole pattern shows the freedom each scheme allows: composite lets the inner zone "float" rotationally locked but translationally free; multi-single-segment pins the inner zone exactly where the basic dimensions put it.

Common mistake

Drawing two ⌖ symbols (one per row) when you intended composite, or vice versa. The difference between the two callouts is one symbol on the FCF — but the inspection result on the same part can pass one and fail the other. ASME Y14.5-2018 is explicit: composite = one symbol shared between rows; multi-single-segment = one symbol per row. If you read a drawing and aren't sure which the designer meant, the symbol's geometry is the legal answer.
